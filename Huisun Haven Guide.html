Cultural Spotlight: The Heritage of Huisun Coffee

A Century of Brewing History

Huisun Forest Recreation Area has been cultivating coffee since the Japanese colonial period, making it the true hometown of Taiwanese coffee. Since 1936, Huisun has been planting Arabica coffee and even won a prestigious silver medal at the World Agricultural Products Tasting Event in the United States.

Exclusive Production & Quality

The current cultivation area spans 11.7 hectares, with an annual yield of 124 kilograms per hectare. This maintains the total production as limited and exclusive. Although its historical halo was somewhat overshadowed by the rise of Gukeng coffee, local tourism operators and agricultural groups are now collaborating with academic institutions to restore Huisun coffee's historical glory.

Revitalizing the Local Industry

With traditional agriculture declining, coffee represents the hope for the industrial vitality of Guoxing Township. Huisun Forest, affiliated with National Chung Hsing University (NCHU), utilizes its nearly 70 years of coffee cultivation technology. NCHU provides professional technical guidance, supplies seedlings, and establishes quality certification systems to unify the brand. Coupled with its rich cultural background, the mass production and proper packaging of "Huisun Coffee" are set to create another boom for Nantou's tourism industry.

Cultural Spotlight: Seediq Weaving Heritage

Floating Weave and Heritage

Floating weave (or weft picking) is commonly found in other Atayal branches, typically applied to cuffs or the hem of garments to highlight uniqueness, especially in wedding attire. For example, in traditional Seediq men's clothing, red yarn is woven into the cuffs of a white plain-weave fabric, decorated with black dotted or linear patterns. Floating weave is essentially extended weft picking, often forming beautiful diamond patterns on a plain weave base.

From the late Qing Dynasty through the Japanese colonial period to the present, traditional Seediq weaving techniques have survived. Although nearly interrupted in the past, the craft began to recover post-war when upright looms were introduced to the tribe to improve the local economy. In the 1990s, government funding encouraged tribal women to learn weaving, bringing it to a cultural peak. Today, tribal elders collaborate with local governments to pass these precious skills down to young weavers.

The Uniqueness of Puniri (Warp Picking)

The Seediq tribe's warp picking technique exhibits unique regional characteristics. While picking techniques are found in other branches, the Seediq warp picking technique, known as Puniri, is registered as a vital cultural asset and is considered the highest level of weaving. Puniri features tightly picked warp threads forming complex, multiple floating diamond patterns. Because the picked warp threads resemble grains of rice, it is commonly referred to as "rice weave" (mini).
